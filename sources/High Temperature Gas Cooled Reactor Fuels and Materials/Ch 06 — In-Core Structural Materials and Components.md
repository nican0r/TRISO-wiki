# Ch 06 — In-Core Structural Materials and Components

Part of [[High Temperature Gas Cooled Reactor Fuels and Materials]] · pp.57–61

**Gist** — A short survey chapter on what the core is built from rather than what it is fuelled with. It makes one structural argument (materials, not fuel, cap achievable outlet temperature), documents the Fort St. Vrain hexagonal block as the reference prismatic geometry, and treats in-core graphite primarily as an irradiation-damage problem requiring grade-by-grade qualification. Ceramic composites are named as the path above 900 °C.

## Key findings

### Materials set the ceiling

- **Materials performance is the limiting factor on achievable gas temperature**, not fuel. Above 950 °C the challenge falls on metallic structural materials, graphitic structures, ceramics and composites (p.57). Japan's HTTR (High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor) has operated at 950 °C (p.57).
- The hexagonal block fuel element was designed at General Atomics in 1965 for Fort St. Vrain, with the goal of a simple geometry adaptable to larger cores (p.57). It became possible only because coated-particle designs had evolved to retain large fractions of fission products — earlier Peach Bottom elements relied on individual helium sweep-gas purging to an external capture system (p.57).
### Block geometry

- **The block geometry serves four purposes simultaneously** (p.58): stacking into approximately cylindrical cores; accommodating control rods in symmetric positions; minimising neutron leakage at the reflector edge; and fitting closely enough to limit coolant bypass flow through inter-block gaps.
- **FSV core layout**: 247 fuel columns, each six blocks high, arrayed within 37 regions — 31 regions of seven columns and six edge regions of five. Each region centred on a pair of control rods suspended by cables from top-head penetrations, plus a flow control valve (p.58).
- **Both principal block dimensions were set by manufacturing limits, not physics** (p.59). Width across flats was capped by the maximum diameter of nuclear-grade graphite log obtainable in the 1960s; length was capped by the drift of small-diameter holes off-centre when drilled from one end.
### Graphite

- **Graphite grade history**: GLCC (Great Lakes Carbon Company) Grade H-327, an extruded needle-coke graphite with anisotropic properties, was used for the initial core and first reload — chosen early for high density, good strength and dimensional stability under irradiation despite the anisotropy. The more isotropic Grade H-451 followed for later reloads and became the reference grade for GA Modular Helium Reactor designs (p.60).
- **Irradiation damage is grade-specific and non-transferable.** Nuclear graphite undergoes radiation-induced creep, shrinking and then swelling at different rates along crystallographic orientations, and its properties depend heavily on source coke and manufacturing process. Each reactor's specific graphite type therefore requires its own irradiation damage testing prior to deployment (p.60).
- **Fuel blocks and reflector blocks age differently.** Fuel blocks are inspected at refuelling and may be reusable if compacts can be removed easily; unfuelled reflector blocks may remain in service for many years and accumulate correspondingly higher damage (p.60).
### Ceramics and manufacture

- **Ceramic composites are the named route above 900 °C.** C/C, SiC/C and SiC/SiC composites maintain structural integrity at high temperature, high fluence and high dpa (displacements per atom), fabricated so that strength is not impacted by irradiation damage. Specific applications: control rod guide tubes, sheathing, and control rod segments themselves; also locations with large thermal gradients, motion, flexure and vibration, and instrumentation housings. Zirconia-based fine-grained superplastic ceramics are suggested where complex shapes are hard to machine from solid ceramic (p.61).
- Manufacturing notes: FSV graphite was machined by GLCC at Morganton, WV, at the point of manufacture, using a GA/GLCC-developed drill capable of one-third of the fuel and coolant holes in a single pass; approximately 30 t of uranium and thorium were processed for the initial core and four reload segments; 10–15 different compact loadings are typically specified per refuelling segment to shape the power distribution (p.60).

## What this chapter does not cover

Nothing on graphite's thermal mass, heat capacity, or its role in passive decay heat removal — despite being the chapter titled for in-core graphite. That material is in §1.1.2 (pp.12–19), not here.

## Open questions raised

- What grade of nuclear graphite is qualified and available today, given that H-451 production ended decades ago? The grade-specific qualification requirement (p.60) makes this a live procurement and licensing question for any new prismatic build.
- The fuel-to-coolant hole ratio in the FSV block (210 fuel : 108 coolant) is a design lever the chapter mentions but does not analyse — "a wide variety of coolant hole-fuel hole patterns to control fuel temperature, core pressure drop and fuel cost" (p.59).
