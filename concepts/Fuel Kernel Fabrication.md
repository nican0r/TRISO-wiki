# Fuel Kernel Fabrication

**In one line** — Fuel kernels are made by sol-gel routes that turn a uranium-bearing liquid into gelled spheres and then shrink and densify them by heat treatment; the route differs for UO₂ and UCO because UCO must carry carbon through the process.

## Why liquid routes

Powder agglomeration came first and can produce monosized kernels from 200 to 1000 µm with porosity controlled between 5% and 20%. Liquid processes displaced it because they can produce oxide, carbide or mixed oxycarbide kernels of almost any desired composition, pore-size distribution and density ([[Ch 02 — Manufacture of TRISO Coated Particles]] pp.23–24).

In a sol-gel process a colloidal liquid of fuel material — the sol, containing oxide crystallites around 50 Å — is dropped into an immiscible liquid, and the near-spherical droplets are solidified by gelation. The fine colloidal distribution is what allows sintering to near-theoretical density at ≥1200 °C in a reducing atmosphere **without applying pressure** (p.24).

## Two routes

**External gelation** (also gel-precipitation, drip-casting) is the reference process for LEU (low-enriched uranium) UO₂ kernels. Gelation is driven from outside: droplets fall through an ammonia atmosphere that precipitates ammonium diuranate as a skin, then into an ammonium hydroxide column where the reaction proceeds inward.

**Internal gelation** is used for UCO. The gelling agent travels inside the droplet — hexamethyltetramine in the broth decomposes in a heated trichloroethylene column and releases ammonia within the droplet — and the broth also carries fine carbon black, which is the carbon that later forms the carbide phases.

## Sphericity is made in flight

In the external gelation route, droplets are shaken from 4–6 nozzles at roughly 100 per second, take spherical shape in free fall by surface tension, and acquire a protective ADU skin from the ammonia atmosphere *before* hitting the liquid — which is what stops them deforming on impact ([[Ch 02 — Manufacture of TRISO Coated Particles]] p.26). In the internal gelation route the same job is done by interfacial tension between the aqueous droplet and the TCE (p.28). Both processes make the sphere before they make the solid.

## Shrinkage and densification, UO₂

| Stage | Diameter | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Cast gelled sphere | ~1.8 mm | ammonium hydroxide column |
| Dried ADU kernel | ~1 mm | age 80 °C, wash water then IPA, vacuum dry 80 °C |
| Calcined kernel | ~750 µm | air to 430 °C; ADU → UO₃ above 300 °C |
| Sintered UO₂ kernel | 500 µm | H₂, reduce 450–650 °C, sinter 1600 °C |

Final density just below the theoretical 10.96 Mg/m³. A factor of 3.6 in diameter, roughly 47× in volume, between cast and finished kernel ([[Ch 02 — Manufacture of TRISO Coated Particles]] pp.26–28).

## Carbon management, UCO

The UCO thermal schedule is organised around not losing the carbon before it is needed. Calcination runs in 100% hydrogen specifically to suppress $2\text{UO}_3 + \text{C} \rightarrow 2\text{UO}_2 + \text{CO}_2$, which would strip substantial carbon, and to suppress the gas shift reaction $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{CO} + \text{H}_2$ by hydrogen overpressure. Carbothermic reduction at 1680 °C then forms the carbides, and densification at 1890 °C runs under CO-Ar to minimise UC formation ([[Ch 02 — Manufacture of TRISO Coated Particles]] pp.28–30).

Typical sintered composition 74 mol% UO₂, 12 mol% UC₂, 14 mol% UC; specification allows 65–85 / 0–27 / 0–35 mol% and is written as limits on O/U and C/U ratios rather than phase fractions (p.30). See [[UCO vs UO2 Kernels]].

## Finishing

Both routes end the same way: sieve to remove undersize and oversize kernels, then sort on a slightly inclined table where spherical kernels roll downhill and odd-shaped ones are vibration-transported perpendicular and collected for recycling. The source describes vibration sorting as "very slow but effective" ([[Ch 02 — Manufacture of TRISO Coated Particles]] p.28).
