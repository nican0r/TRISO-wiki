1. Introduction
TRISO coated particle fuel is a key component of HTGRs and has
been shown to be the most robust ‘standard’ nuclear fuel produced when
considered as a function of fission product release in an accident sce-
nario or during normal operation (Powers and Wirth, 2010). It consists
of a fissile kernel of material surrounded by successive layers of PyC and
a dense SiC layer, which acts in place of a cladding to retain fission
products and provide protection to the fissile material. In practice the
carbon layers at either side of the SiC barrier are present to protect the
integrity of the SiC, both from irradiation damage and gaseous swelling
from the uranium kernel, and from mechanical damage during manu-
facture in the case of the outer PyC layer (Morris et al., 2004).

2. Background
2.1. HTGR and TRISO coated fuel particles
The design of coated fuel particle was initially proposed during the
early stages of The Dragon Project (Price, 1977). There have been many
variations in the design of coated particle fuels, with the three most
prominent being laminar, BISO and TRISO, for which schematic draw-
ings are shown in Fig. 1(Stansfield, 1991). The “laminar” design was
used in initial cores for a few early HTGR projects (Peach Bottom, AVR
and Dragon), but was abandoned due to issues relating to fission recoil
damage and fission product release (Goeddel, 1967). BISO particles
added a porous low density PyC layer (known as “buffer”) to retain
fission products and limit damage from their recoil (Gulden and Nickel,
1977). The most recent established design is that of TRISO coated par-
ticles, which adds a SiC layer and outer high-density PyC layer, and it is
capable of retaining metallic fission product at elevated temperatures
(Gulden and Nickel, 1977).
For TRISO coated fuel particles each of the four layers plays a
different role during fabrication, handling and in-reactor operation. The
buffer layer is a porous PyC layer with density around 1.1 g/cm3, half of
the following PyC layer. The buffer is designed with a thickness around
100 μm to attenuate fission product recoil, accommodate fission gas
release and kernel swelling (Verfondern (eds.), 1997). The inner
high-density PyC layer prevents hydrogen chloride ingress to the kernel
during the SiC coating process, provides a dense surface for SiC depo-
sition, retains fission products and maintain the integrity of the SiC layer
under irradiation-induced shrinkage. The SiC layer maintains the
structural integrity of the particle, accommodates internal gas pressure
and acts as primary fission product barrier. Lastly, the outermost
high-density PyC layer protects the SiC during particle handling and
from external chemical reactions, compresses the SiC layer during
irradiation and is an additional barrier to fission product release (Morris
et al., 2004). When TRISO coated fuel particles are consolidated in
carbonaceous fuel matrix material into pebbles or prismatic block, the
OPyC layer can also provide a bonding surface (Demkowicz et al., 2019).

SiC is the critical cladding component of in the TRISO coated particle
and the focus of the present work. Its in-reactor performance is impor-

layer producing TRISO coated fuel particles with high-quality SiC is
essential. Properties such as stoichiometry, microstructure and density
are closely linked to in-reactor performance of TRISO coated particles.
Stoichiometric SiC with the ideal density of 3.2 g/cm3 is thought to be
ideal for mechanical strength and to provide a hermetic seal for fission
gas retention (Price, 1977; Stinton and Lackey, 1977). Understanding
what constitutes idea microstructural characteristics can be complicated
by the need to manage both active silver diffusion and palladium
corrosion behaviours, and conflicting data. For example, while fine
grain SiC has been shown to provide better retention in studies by Petti
et al. (2003), Gerczak et al. (2016)found that samples with finer grained
SiC had higher silver release rates at elevated temperature.
2.2. FB-CVD process
The FB-CVD process is used for coating uranic kernels to produce
TRISO fuel. The terms “fluidised bed” and “spouted bed” refer to the
circulating motions of kernels when fluidising gas enters the coating
furnace. The Geldart classification is used to predict important fluid-
isation parameters such as minimal fluidisation velocity. As can be seen
in Fig. 2, uranic kernels are considered ‘Group D’ particles due to their
high density and large diameter (Geldart, 1972, 1973). Bed expansion is
a fluidisation parameter which refers to the volume increase of a bed of
granular media caused by gaseous up-flow and is poor for group D
particles which means that maintaining a high gas-solid contact effi-
ciency is difficult (Vahlas et al., 2006; Vanni et al., 2015). For these
reasons a conical fluidised bed CVD was developed (Vahlas et al., 2006;
Yang et al., 2017). The main distinction between a conventional FB-CVD
coater and a conical FB-CVD coater is the design of the coating furnace
(shown in Fig. 3). In a conventional FB-CVD system, the bottom of the
coating furnace is mostly flat with a large gas distribution grid, whereas
for conical FB-CVD systems a cone-shaped (or a bowl-shape) base is
machined, with a gas distributor in forms such as a “showerhead” (with
either a single-inlet or multi-inlet) (Liu et al., 2015a) or a distribution
plate (Stinton and Lackey, 1977).
Depending on the fluidising velocity and column height the particle
behaviour during fluidisation can be divided generally into four re-
gimes, although some authors divide into five with alternative names
(Epstein and Grace (eds.), 2010, Yan et al., 2024). The four generally
accepted regimes are: static bed, bubbling, slugging and spouting. At the
start of fluidisation with a low gas flow rate (where annular gas velocity
is less than the minimum fluidising velocity) a heavy particle bed re-
mains immobile. As annular gas velocity increases the particle bed en-
ters the stable spouting zone, which is characterised by the formation of
a fountain in the middle of the bed. This is the preferred fluidisation
mode, as there is only a small chance of particle dwelling (e.g. remaining
within a small location within the chamber) (Marshall, 2017). As the

3.2. Grain growth mechanism
The grain growth of SiC has been discussed extensively for conven-
tional CVD processes such as those involving chimney and horizontal
CVD coaters (Cheng et al., 1987; Choi and Kim, 1991), but the number of
studies on grain growth in a TRISO coated particle context is limited. A
data gap exists in linking the deposition mechanism under various
processing condition and grain development in SiC for TRISO coated
fuel particles.
Lo´pez-Honorato (2008) proposed a SiC grain growth model to
explain both the formation of porosity and the effects of deposition
temperature and precursor concentrations. As shown in Fig. 10 the
growth process can be divided into three stages: (a) Nuclei formation,

liquid droplets on the surface of the substrate fuse and crystalise, while
at higher temperatures SiC micro-crystals are formed directly after py-

4. Parametric review on SiC deposition conditions
4.1. Stoichiometry
SiC occurs in two polytypes: α-SiC and β-SiC. α-SiC is characterised
by a hexagonal crystal structure and is formed at temperatures greater
than 1700 ◦C. β-SiC has a cubic crystal structure and is formed at tem-
peratures below 1700 ◦C. In the nuclear industry β-SiC is preferred for its
isotropic properties, which lead to improved radiation tolerance (Parish
et al., 2017). In most cases β-SiC is deposited as the single phase from
temperature of 1300 ◦C–1700 ◦C (Minato and Fukuda, 1987;
Lo´pez-Honorato et al., 2008; Cromarty, 2012; Yang et al., 2019a).
Various dilutants have been trialled alongside MTS including argon,
hydrogen and nitrogen. When hydrogen is the only diluent gas used a
number of TRISO studies have found that deposition temperatures of
1500 ◦C–1600 ◦C can produce stoichiometric SiC (Foerthmann et al.,
1973; Krautwasser et al., 1983; Minato and Fukuda, 1987;
Lo´pez-Honorato et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2009; Cromarty, 2012; Yang
et al., 2019a). Below or above this temperature range excessive Si or C is
co-deposited within the SiC structure (Foerthmann et al., 1973; Kraut-
wasser et al., 1983; Minato and Fukuda, 1987; Lo´pez-Honorato et al.,
2008; Kim et al., 2009; Cromarty, 2012; Yang et al., 2019a). This
behaviour is generally accepted for the temperature range in question,
although the exact boundary between the SiC-Si to SiC, and SiC to SiC-C

4.3. Density
Deposition temperature is commonly believed to closely relate to
deposited material density, and has been shown to peak between
1500 ◦C and 1600 ◦C (Federer, 1977b; Stinton and Lackey, 1977; Lauf

Marshall, 2008; Kim et al., 2009; Cromarty, 2012). The presence of
excess Si is thought to be one of the driving mechanisms behind low
density SiC formation. As discussed above, deposited SiC is generally
thought to be stoichiometric between 1500 ◦C and 1600 ◦C. Above this
range a reduction in SiC density has also been noted (Federer, 1977a;
Stinton and Lackey, 1977; Lauf and Braski, 1981; Xu et al., 1995; Cro-
marty, 2012; Hancke et al., 2012).

4.4. Coating rate
Coating rate (or deposition rate) is an important economic factor
which can limit total throughput as well as influence SiC material
properties. In the past decades a variety of studies have investigated the

1968; Stinton and Lackey, 1977). There have been efforts in modelling

report the coating rate. It is complex to compare published values in
different units without additional context, and as μm/min is the unit
used in most published work, the authors have disregarded data not
presented in μm/min for this section.

Deposition temperature is commonly agreed to be the most influ-

rapidly with increasing temperature. On the other hand, Cromarty

concentration and particle charges on efficiency. Below 1400 ◦C an in-
crease in MTS concentration resulted in a reduction in deposition effi-

MTS supply results in more MTS exhausted from the system. Likewise
the effect of kernel charge is more pronounced than at higher temper-
atures, as additional surface area is available on which to deposit. As
temperature rises, the influence of MTS concentration gradually de-
creases as the rate limiting factor becomes available kernel surface area,
and MTS reacts almost completely within the chamber.

deposition temperature, beyond deposition temperature around

ratio are referred to in several manuscripts and are all in fact varying the
relative concentration of the reactive substances and the diluent. When
H2/MTS ratio is discussed in literature the gas flow rate and its relevance
to the size of the coating system is often overlooked.
There are several reasons why efficiency can be reduced, for example
that less than optimum deposition temperature and gas composition
were used for the run, or as a result of the decreased residency time due
to the high flow rates (Federer, 1977a).
Reaction surface is a parameter that is decided by the total batch
charge. Gulden (1968)’s study varied deposition temperature, carrier

compared the results with a previous study done on a 5” (12.7 cm)
coater, in which the author concluded that a small charge could be
insufficient to fill the reaction zone in the coater, and the lack of
available reaction surface is the cause of the low efficiency observed. In
Stinton and Lackey (1977)’s work, it was reported that a coating run in a
24 cm diameter coater has higher efficiency, which agrees with Gyar-
mati and Nickel (1972)’s observation that increasing coater size in-
creases efficiency.

research papers cross six decades, general trends of the effects of fabri-
cation parameters on SiC layer of the TRISO coated particle properties
can be concluded:
• Stoichiometry: SiC generally remains stoichiometric under deposi-
tion temperature roughly from 1500 to 1600 ◦C, excess silicon or
carbon appears when temperature drops below or rises above the
stoichiometric interval. When argon is present in the system, this
stoichiometric interval can shift towards lower temperatures. The
H2/MTS ratio is also reported to influence the Si:C ratio, however,
there is a lack of consistency on this effect in available data.
• Surface morphology: When deposition is within or close to the stoi-
chiometric interval, faceted features can be seen on the surface of the
deposited SiC layer. Botryoidal shapes or pyramid shapes are seen at
deposition temperature below or above the stoichiometric interval.
Grain size increases with the deposition temperature, which may
cause porosity in the SiC. When the coating gas is very concentrated
or very diluted, a reduction in grain size is seen. The addition of
argon can refine the grain size further.
• Cross-section microscopy: At low deposition temperature (below the
stochiometric interval), striations are shown. As temperature rises,
grains develop between the striation boundaries, enlarge and form
bands of crystals. Above 1500 ◦C, the grain became micron-level and
disturb the striation boundaries. Increasing the H2/MTS ratio has
been shown to promote grain growth, which cause the early devel-
opment of the bands of crystals.
• Density: High-density SiC (above 3.1 g/cm3) has been achieved at a
range of deposition temperatures. Within the stoichiometric interval
SiC density peaks. It has been reported that density is associated with
H2/MTS ratio, however, when data are compiled, no clear trend can
be seen.
• Deposition rate: deposition rate and MTS flux have a linear rela-
tionship, and the relationship is broadly consistent between the four
FB-CVD coaters for which it has been reported. The relationship
between deposition rate, H2/MTS ratio and deposition temperature
had been reported before, but it is not clear in the aggregated data.
Deposition rate is also reported to relate to the SiC density and SiC
strength.
• Coating efficiency: When deposition temperature increases, pro-
cessing efficiency generally increases although the relationship also
becomes less direct. In some data, process efficiency has achieved
100 % within the uncertainty of the measurements used which are
themselves non-trivial. H2/MTS was reported to impact the process
efficiency, however, the compiled data does not show a clear
relationship.
With the conclusions above, coarse predictions on TRISO coated
particle properties can be made with known fabrication parameters. For
a generic lab-scale coater, deposition temperature should be the most
important fabrication parameter. The deposition temperature should be
from 1500 to 1600 ◦C to produce a SiC layer with satisfactory density
and stoichiometry. The addition of argon should also be considered to
produce fine-grain SiC to prevent fission production migration.
The effect of the H2/MTS ratio is not well understood, which may be
due to the fact that H2/MTS is an indirect variable to compare MTS
content and H2 content. Without a direct measurement of the substances
(such as MTS flux) alongside reported ratios, it is difficult to compare
between datasets to draw meaningful conclusions.
The CVD coater analysed in this paper all have different designs, and
coating chamber diameter is a useful way to differentiate them from
each other. Depending on the scale of the fabrication, gas flow rate and
coating gas ratio requirement are very different, and achieve desired
fluidisation is another challenge. The large data spread in the quanti-
tative comparisons suggests that an underlying understanding of the
coating parameters and TRISO properties are needed.
